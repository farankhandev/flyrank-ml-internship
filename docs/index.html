```
Machine Learning for Prioritizing Declining Content

A practical experiment on whether machine learning can help identify content that may need attention when its search performance starts to decline.

Faran Khan · Machine Learning · FlyRank ML Internship · 2026

```

```
Abstract

I wanted to test a fairly practical question: can machine learning help a team decide which declining content to look at first?

For this experiment, I used historical impressions and query-level signals from the FlyRank internship warehouse and trained a Random Forest classifier to rank content that showed signs of declining search performance. I compared it with a Logistic Regression baseline using a client-level holdout split.

The Random Forest achieved a ROC-AUC of 0.604, compared with 0.504 for the baseline. At the top of the ranking, it reached 0.880 Precision@50.

These results suggest that the model may be useful for prioritizing pages for human review. They do not show that changing one of those pages will improve its future search performance. I therefore treat the model as a prioritization experiment rather than a production forecasting system.

1. Problem

Research Question

Can machine learning help prioritize content items that show signs of declining search performance?

The motivation was simple. A team may have a large number of pages to review, while the amount of time available for manual analysis is limited. Rather than treating every page equally, I wanted to see whether a model could produce a useful ranking for deciding where to start.

The model is therefore meant to support a human reviewer, not replace one. A high model score means that an item is worth investigating; it does not automatically mean that the page needs to be changed.

2. Data

Data Source

I used the FlyRank internship warehouse full release hosted on Hugging Face.

The main tables used in the analysis were:

  • dim_clients — client-level metadata and available history dates.
  • dim_content — content-level metadata.
  • fact_content_daily_performance — daily Google Search Console performance data.
  • fact_content_query_90d — content and query-level signals aggregated over the available 90-day window.

Modeling Window

I used a 60-day performance window split into two 30-day periods. The previous 30 days were used as the historical reference, while the latest 30 days were used to determine whether performance had declined.

To avoid including very low-volume content, an item needed at least 100 impressions in the previous 30-day period to be included.

This resulted in 111,247 content items with enough performance history. After removing rows with missing values in the selected features, 102,203 items remained for modeling.

No client names, domains, URLs, search queries, or other private identifying information are included in this public analysis.

3. Methodology

Defining Declining Content

I defined an item as declining when its impressions in the latest 30-day period were below 80% of its impressions in the previous 30-day period. In practical terms, this represents a decline of more than 20%.

Features

I used five available signals as model features:

  • Previous 30-day impressions (imp_prev30)
  • Visible query count (visible_queries)
  • Rare-query impression share (rare_share)
  • Anonymized-query impression share (anon_share)
  • Top-query impression share (top_query_share)

Models

The main model was a Random Forest Classifier with 200 trees and a fixed random seed of 42.

For comparison, I used Logistic Regression as the baseline. Both models used the same features and were evaluated on the same test split.

Validation

I used GroupShuffleSplit to create a client-level holdout. Approximately 75% of the modeling data was used for training and 25% for testing, with clients kept separate between the two sets.

The final test set contained 51,702 rows from 13 clients.

Evaluation

The main use case here is ranking. If a team can only investigate a limited number of pages, the quality of the first few predictions matters more than simply getting the largest possible overall classification accuracy.

I therefore looked at:

  • ROC-AUC
  • Precision@50
  • Precision@100
  • Precision@500

Leakage Consideration

The decline label was calculated from the latest 30-day impressions relative to the previous 30-day impressions.

There is an important limitation in the available query-level data. The query signals came from the available 90-day query table, which can overlap with the period used to define the outcome. That means some features may contain information from the same period as the outcome.

Because of this, I would not describe these results as evidence of a leakage-free production forecasting model. I treat them as an experiment showing whether these signals can help with prioritization.

The Random Forest achieved a ROC-AUC of 0.604, compared with 0.504 for Logistic Regression.

The difference becomes more interesting when looking at the highest-ranked items. Among the top 50 predictions, the Random Forest reached 0.880 Precision@50. In the test set, that means 88% of those 50 items were labeled as declining.

The overall accuracy result was different. The Random Forest achieved 61.3% accuracy, while Logistic Regression reached 65.8%.

I would not use the accuracy result alone to choose between these models because the intended use of the experiment is ranking. The main question was whether the model could put a useful group of potentially declining pages near the top of the review queue.

5. Interpretation & Limitations

Feature Importance

Feature Importance
Rare-query impression share 0.236
Anonymized-query impression share 0.233
Previous 30-day impressions 0.222
Top-query impression share 0.192
Visible query count 0.117

Rare-query impression share had the highest feature importance at 0.236, followed closely by anonymized-query impression share at 0.233. Previous 30-day impressions contributed 0.222.

These values are useful for understanding what the Random Forest relied on when making its decisions, but they should not be read as causal effects. A higher feature importance does not mean that changing that feature would cause content performance to improve.

Limitations

  • The client-level holdout makes the evaluation more demanding and provides a better test of whether the model can generalize across clients.
  • The model predicts the defined declining-impressions label. It does not establish that refreshing a page will cause its search performance to improve.
  • The dataset is anonymized, so the results should not be treated as evidence about any particular company or website.
  • The features used here do not capture every factor that can influence search performance, including changes in search intent, competition, and search-engine behavior.
  • The available 90-day query data may overlap with the outcome period, which creates a potential temporal leakage concern.
  • Results from this experiment may not generalize equally well to future datasets or different clients.

Taken together, these limitations mean that I would use this model as a decision-support and prioritization tool. The final decision about whether a page actually needs to be changed should remain with a human reviewer.

6. Ranked Recommendations

1. Start with the highest-ranked pages

If review capacity is limited, the first place to start is the top of the model's ranking. The 0.880 Precision@50 result suggests that this small group contains a high concentration of content labeled as declining.

2. Check the signals behind the score

I would not treat the model score as the final answer. Before taking action, a reviewer should look at the historical impressions and available query-level signals that contributed to the ranking.

3. Review the actual content

The next step should still be human review. Things such as search intent, content quality, freshness, competition, and whether the page still answers the user's needs can provide context that these five model features cannot.

4. Use the ranking to focus limited resources

A team may not have enough time to manually inspect every page. A ranked list provides a practical way to focus that effort on a smaller group of pages first.

5. Measure the outcome after a change

If a page is refreshed, its search performance should be monitored afterward. The model can help decide where to investigate, but the experiment itself does not show that a particular refresh will work.

Model ranking → Human review → Content decision → Refresh/test → Monitor
```
