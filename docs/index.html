Machine Learning for Prioritizing Declining Content

A machine learning study evaluating whether historical search-performance data can help prioritize content showing signs of decline for human review.

Abstract

This project examines a practical content-review problem: when a team is responsible for a large number of pages, how can it decide which pages should be reviewed first when search performance begins to decline? Using the FlyRank internship dataset, I tested whether historical search-performance data could help rank content showing signs of decline.

I compared a Random Forest classifier with a Logistic Regression baseline using five client-grouped validation splits. Across the splits, the Random Forest achieved a mean ROC-AUC of 0.6164, compared with 0.6112 for Logistic Regression. Its mean Precision@100 was 0.8200, compared with 0.7280 for the baseline.

The results suggest that historical performance features may help prioritize content for human review. However, the improvement in mean ROC-AUC over the baseline is modest, and the results do not establish that changing a page will improve its future search performance. The model is therefore treated as a decision-support tool rather than an automated content decision system.

1. Problem

Case study framing: This paper uses the FlyRank content dataset to study how historical search-performance data can help prioritize content for human review. The objective is to identify which items deserve attention first, rather than automatically deciding which pages should be changed.

Research Question

Can machine learning use historical search-performance data to prioritize content items showing signs of declining impressions?

A team responsible for many pages may not have enough time to investigate every change in search performance. Identifying a possible decline is only the first step; the team also needs a practical way to decide where its review effort should begin.

This study evaluates whether four historical performance features can help rank content items according to the defined decline label. The experiment uses April and May 2026 data to construct historical features and June 2026 performance to define the observed outcome.

The intended workflow is to prioritize the highest-ranked items, investigate their historical performance, and let a human reviewer decide whether further analysis or a content change is appropriate.

The model does not establish the cause of a decline or guarantee that refreshing a page will improve rankings, impressions, or traffic.

2. Data

Data Source

The analysis uses the FlyRank internship warehouse full release hosted on Hugging Face . The study uses daily content-performance data for April, May, and June 2026 to construct historical features and evaluate the defined decline outcome.

Observation Periods

April and May 2026 provide the historical performance period. June 2026 provides the outcome period. Historical impressions and clicks are used to construct the model features, while the number of historical observation days helps describe the available history.

Impressions are normalized to a 30-day basis so that content items with different amounts of observed historical data can be compared using a common time scale.

After applying the historical-data and observation requirements, 91,033 content items from 43 clients remained eligible for modeling.

This public-facing analysis intentionally excludes client names, domains, URLs, search queries, and other private identifying information.

3. Methodology

Defining Declining Content

A content item was labeled as declining when its June 2026 normalized 30-day impressions were below 80% of its normalized historical 30-day impressions calculated from April and May 2026. This represents a decline of more than 20% relative to the historical baseline.

Features

The model used four historical performance features:

  • Historical impressions (hist_impressions)
  • Historical clicks (hist_clicks)
  • Number of historical observation days (hist_days)
  • Normalized historical 30-day impressions (historical_30d_impressions)

These features summarize the available historical performance. No query-level features are used in this experiment.

Models

The main model was a Random Forest Classifier with 200 trees and random state 42. It was used to evaluate whether historical performance signals could distinguish content items labeled as declining.

Logistic Regression served as the baseline model. Both models used the same four features and were evaluated using the same client-grouped validation setup.

Validation

I used GroupShuffleSplit with five splits, a 25% test fraction, and random state 123, grouping by client ID. Each split held out 11 clients so that a client's records did not appear in both training and test sets within that split. Test-set sizes varied across the five splits, and test sets may overlap between splits.

Client-level grouping is important because records from the same client can share characteristics. Keeping each client's records within a single partition in each split reduces the risk of evaluating the models on other records from clients seen during training.

Evaluation

Results were evaluated across the five validation splits. Mean ROC-AUC measures how well each model ranks declining items above non-declining items. Mean accuracy measures the proportion of correctly classified items at the selected classification threshold. Mean Precision@100 measures the proportion of declining items among the 100 highest-ranked test predictions.

  • Mean ROC-AUC
  • Mean accuracy
  • Mean Precision@100

Interpretation of the Validation Results

The reported metrics are averages across the five validation splits. Because different clients are held out in different splits, the results reflect performance across multiple client-level holdouts. Test sets may overlap across splits, so the five evaluations should not be interpreted as five completely independent datasets.

Leakage Consideration

The model uses historical performance features from April and May 2026 and evaluates the defined decline outcome using June 2026 normalized impressions. This separation is intended to keep the feature period earlier than the outcome period.

The validity of this separation depends on the actual feature construction and data-processing steps. The implementation should ensure that no June outcome information enters the historical features, preprocessing, or model selection.

The results should be treated as an evaluation of this particular dataset and validation procedure, not as proof of production forecasting performance.

The Random Forest achieved a mean ROC-AUC of 0.6164, compared with 0.6112 for Logistic Regression. The difference is modest, indicating that both models had similar overall ranking performance across the five validation splits.

The difference was larger for mean Precision@100. The Random Forest achieved 0.8200, compared with 0.7280 for Logistic Regression. This suggests that, under the evaluated setup, the Random Forest's top-ranked items contained a higher proportion of items labeled as declining.

Mean accuracy was 0.6642 for the Random Forest and 0.6571 for Logistic Regression. This is a smaller difference than the one observed for Precision@100. Since the goal is to prioritize a limited number of items for review, the ranking metrics are particularly relevant to the intended use.

The metrics reported above are means across five validation splits. Precision@100 is a ranking metric and should not be interpreted as the probability that an arbitrary content item is declining.

5. Interpretation & Limitations

Feature Importance

The following values are feature importances from the final Random Forest fit on the full eligible dataset. They are not averages across the five validation splits.

Feature Importance
Historical impressions (hist_impressions) 0.4032
Normalized historical 30-day impressions (historical_30d_impressions) 0.3711
Historical clicks (hist_clicks) 0.1520
Historical observation days (hist_days) 0.0737

Historical impressions had the highest reported importance at 0.4032, followed by normalized historical 30-day impressions at 0.3711. Historical clicks contributed 0.1520, while historical observation days contributed 0.0737.

These values describe the model's relative use of the features. They do not establish that a feature causes a decline, nor do they show what would happen if a feature or a page were changed.

Limitations

  • The mean ROC-AUC improvement over Logistic Regression is modest, so the overall ranking advantage should not be overstated.
  • The model predicts the defined declining-impressions label. It does not demonstrate that a content refresh will cause performance to improve.
  • Client-level holdouts provide a more demanding evaluation than randomly splitting individual records, but performance may vary for clients not represented in the evaluated dataset.
  • Test sets can overlap between validation splits. The mean metrics summarize these splits but do not represent five independent datasets.
  • The available historical features do not capture every factor affecting search performance, including search intent, competition, content quality, and search-engine behavior.
  • The decline label depends on the chosen 20% threshold and the historical observation requirements. Different definitions could produce different results.
  • Results from this experiment may not generalize equally to future periods, other datasets, or all clients.

Overall, the model should be treated as a decision-support and prioritization tool. A human reviewer should make the final decision about whether a page actually needs to be changed.

6. Ranked Recommendations

1. Start with the highest-ranked pages

When review capacity is limited, begin with the pages at the top of the model's ranking. The mean Precision@100 result suggests that the highest-ranked group contained a greater concentration of declining items than the Logistic Regression baseline under the evaluated setup.

2. Examine historical performance

Use historical impressions, clicks, observation days, and normalized historical impressions to understand the available performance context before making a decision.

3. Review the content itself

A model score should be a starting point for investigation. A human reviewer should consider search intent, content quality, freshness, competition, and whether the page still answers users' needs.

4. Use ranking to allocate limited resources

Teams do not always have enough time to review every page. A ranked list can help direct limited review capacity toward a smaller group of higher-priority items.

5. Monitor outcomes after changes

If a page is refreshed, monitor its search performance afterward. Where practical, use an appropriate comparison or controlled evaluation to assess whether the change was associated with an improvement. The ranking model alone cannot establish causality.

Model ranking → Human review → Content decision → Refresh/test → Monitor

7. Reproducibility

The project repository contains the notebooks, scripts, documentation, and analysis artifacts used for this work. The main experiment uses the following configuration:

  • Eligible dataset: 91,033 items from 43 clients
  • Features: Four historical performance features
  • Validation: Five client-grouped splits
  • Test fraction: 25% per split
  • Random Forest: 200 trees, random state 42
  • Logistic Regression: Baseline model

The validation procedure uses random state 123 for GroupShuffleSplit and groups records by client ID. Each split holds out 11 clients, and test-set sizes vary across the splits.

The feature-importance values are from the final Random Forest fit on the full eligible dataset, rather than averages across validation splits. They are reported separately from the validation metrics.
